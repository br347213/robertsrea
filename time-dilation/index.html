03 · Why it happens

Light is the clue.

Everyone measures light moving at the same speed. That strange fact forces space and time to adjust.

A simple “light clock”

Imagine a clock that ticks every time a pulse of light bounces between two mirrors.

Clock beside you
Same clock moving past you

Why the diagonal path matters

From your point of view, the moving clock’s light has farther to travel between ticks because the whole clock is sliding sideways.

But light is not allowed to simply go faster. Its speed must still be the speed of light. So a longer path must take more time. That is the intuition behind the moving clock ticking more slowly relative to you.
The traveler does not feel slowed down. Their heartbeat, thoughts, watch, and aging all feel completely normal to them.

05 · The confusing parts

Questions that usually trip people up.

Wouldn’t each person say the other one is moving?

During steady relative motion, yes. The situation is symmetric. The twin round trip is different because the traveler changes inertial frames when turning around and coming back. When they reunite, both can compare their clocks at the same place and directly see the accumulated difference.

Does time dilation stop if the spaceship stops?

The extra time dilation from relative speed stops once the traveler is no longer moving relative to Earth. But the age difference already accumulated does not disappear. If one is now five years younger, they stay five years younger unless later motion or gravity creates another difference.

Is this just what the light looks like from far away?

No. Delayed light signals can affect what you see, but relativistic time dilation remains after you mathematically correct for signal travel time. Real clocks flown on aircraft and satellites accumulate measurably different amounts of time.

What about gravity?

Gravity can also change elapsed time. Clocks deeper in a gravitational field run differently from clocks higher up. That is gravitational time dilation, described by general relativity rather than the velocity-only example on this page.

Optional math

The slider is using one equation.

You do not need this equation to understand the page. It just calculates the traveler’s elapsed time.

traveler time = Earth time × √(1 − v²/c²)
v is the traveler’s speed and c is the speed of light. As v gets closer to c, the square-root term gets smaller, so less time accumulates for the traveler.
